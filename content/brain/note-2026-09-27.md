There's a procurement philosophy that sounds rational on paper: establish minimum technical requirements, then select the lowest-priced vendor that meets them. Lowest Price Technically Acceptable. LPTA.

I've watched this approach hollow out operational capability across dozens of stations.

The problem isn't the logic — it's the assumption that "technically acceptable" captures everything that matters. It doesn't. It captures everything you thought to specify. There's a canyon between those two things.

Ground handling contracts are my recurring lesson in this. The spec says vehicles must be serviceable. It doesn't say anything about driver attitude when a connection is tight. It says equipment must be available. It doesn't capture whether the supervisor answers the phone at 3am when weather diverts six aircraft to your station simultaneously.

When you optimise purely for price at the floor of acceptability, you get vendors who have optimised their entire operation to deliver exactly that floor. Every margin has been cut. There's no slack in the system. The first time you need something beyond the literal contract language, you discover you've purchased a brittle partner.

The alternative isn't ignoring price — that's negligent in its own way. It's being honest about what "technically acceptable" actually means for your operation. Sometimes that means paying for capability you hope you'll never use. Sometimes it means weighting factors that are harder to score: track record during disruption, investment in their own people, how they handled their last failure.

I've started asking vendors a simple question: "Tell me about the last time you went beyond contract scope for a customer, and why."

The ones who struggle to answer have already told me everything I need to know.
