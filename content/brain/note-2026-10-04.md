I've investigated enough ground incidents to know where the fault lines actually are. It's rarely one person making a catastrophic mistake. It's almost always the seam between two people, two shifts, two departments.

The handover problem.

A turnaround runs perfectly until the cabin crew assumes the ground team knows about the wheelchair passenger in 32A. The ground team assumes it's in the system. Nobody confirms. Flight delays. Customer stranded on the jet bridge.

When I build agent systems at home, I obsess over handover protocols. What happens when Agent A completes a task and Agent B needs to pick it up? What's the minimum viable context that needs to transfer? What happens if B is unavailable?

In aviation ops, we use standardised briefings, checklists, formal handover logs. But the failure mode isn't the absence of process — it's assuming the process was followed. It's the supervisor who "knew" the message got passed because it always does.

Three things I've learned:

**Explicit beats implicit.** If the receiving party hasn't acknowledged, the handover hasn't happened. This applies to agents, to shift supervisors, to email threads.

**Context degrades.** The longer the chain of handovers, the more critical information gets stripped away. Fight this with structured formats that force completeness.

**Handovers are emotional.** People leaving a shift are mentally checked out. People arriving are still warming up. Neither are at peak attention. Design your systems assuming this, not despite it.

The best operations I've seen don't just train people on their jobs — they train them specifically on the five minutes before and after they're responsible. That's where everything breaks.

Whether you're managing 83 stations or three AI agents, the principle holds: you're only as reliable as your worst handover.
