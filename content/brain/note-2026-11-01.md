Most improvement frameworks obsess over big moves. The career pivot. The restructure. The bold bet.

But managing 83 stations has taught me something different: the teams that outperform don't make bigger decisions—they make smaller adjustments more frequently.

I've started applying this to everything. Every Sunday evening, I run a 15-minute calibration. Not a full review. Not goal-setting. Just three questions:

1. What friction did I notice this week that I worked around instead of fixing?
2. Where did I spend energy that produced no signal?
3. What's one micro-adjustment I can make in the next seven days?

The answers are almost always small. Move a recurring meeting 30 minutes earlier because energy drops after lunch. Stop cc'ing someone on a thread they never read. Adjust the prompt on an agent that's returning slightly wrong outputs.

Individually, meaningless. Compounded over 52 weeks, transformative.

The trap most leaders fall into—myself included, for years—is waiting for enough friction to justify a "proper" intervention. By then, the problem has calcified. You're doing surgery when a bandage would have worked months ago.

This is also why I think continuous deployment works in software and should work in leadership. Small changes shipped fast, monitored for regression, rolled back if needed. The alternative—quarterly reorganisations, annual strategy refreshes—creates change debt that becomes impossible to service.

The hard part isn't the calibration itself. It's building the muscle to notice friction in the first place. Most of us adapt so quickly that inefficiency becomes invisible within weeks.

The question I keep coming back to: what am I currently tolerating that I would never have accepted six months ago?

Usually, the answer is uncomfortable.
