I've sat through hundreds of procurement cycles. Ground handling contracts, fuel agreements, equipment purchases, tech vendors. And I've noticed something that took years to articulate: most procurement processes are designed to produce defensible decisions, not good ones.

The theater looks like this: RFPs with 47 weighted criteria. Evaluation matrices color-coded to three decimal places. Steering committees that meet monthly to review scores nobody truly believes in. Everyone performs rigor. Nobody takes risk.

The result? You end up with the vendor who's best at responding to RFPs, not the vendor who's best at solving your problem. These are almost never the same company.

In ground operations, this matters enormously. A handling agent who looks perfect on paper — certifications, fleet size, client logos — can still crater your turnaround times because their supervisors are stretched across too many flights. Meanwhile, the smaller operator with hungry leadership and actual presence on the ramp gets screened out at Stage 1 because their documentation wasn't polished enough.

What I've learned to do: front-load the process with operational questions that can't be gamed. Not "describe your quality management system" but "walk me through what happens when a wheelchair passenger is left waiting." Not "list your technology capabilities" but "show me your supervisor's actual screen during a delay."

The uncomfortable truth is that procurement rigor and procurement theater often look identical. The difference is whether anyone in the room has enough operational knowledge — and enough authority — to override the matrix when it's obviously wrong.

I've pushed back on "winning" bids three times in my career. Twice, I was right. Once, I wasn't. But all three times, the process got better because someone was willing to own an actual opinion.
