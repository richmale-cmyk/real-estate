I've watched more procurement exercises collapse from internal dysfunction than from supplier failure.

The pattern is predictable. You start with a clear requirement: ground support equipment for six stations, specific lift capacity, standard safety certifications. The RFP goes out. Then the creep begins.

Engineering wants telemetry integration. Finance wants a leasing structure that wasn't in scope. One station manager remembers a problem from 2019 and demands a feature that addresses it. Safety adds three new certifications that only two vendors globally can meet. IT insists on API compatibility with a system you're replacing next year anyway.

By the time you've consolidated "stakeholder input," your clean specification has become a fantasy document. The vendors who could deliver at your budget withdraw. The ones who remain either can't actually meet the inflated requirements or have priced in the chaos premium.

The discipline I've learned: freeze specifications at 80% of what everyone wants. That last 20% is where projects go to die. It's where timelines triple, costs balloon, and you end up with a bespoke solution that only one supplier can maintain — forever.

When I run procurement now, I separate the "must have" conversation from the "should have" conversation by at least a week. People argue differently when they've had time to distinguish genuine operational requirements from wish-list items.

The uncomfortable truth is that most specification creep comes from people protecting themselves. Adding requirements is risk-free for the person adding them. If something goes wrong later, they can say they raised it. The cost is distributed across the project; the cover is personal.

Good procurement leadership means protecting the scope from your own organisation.
