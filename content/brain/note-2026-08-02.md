I've run enough RFPs to know the dirty secret of procurement: the incumbent wins most rebids not because they're best, but because switching costs feel insurmountable to the people who'd have to execute the transition.

Ground handling contracts are a perfect example. You'll have a vendor delivering 75% of what you need, charging 20% more than market rate, and when you put it to tender, your station managers will find seventeen reasons why the cheaper, better-rated competitor "won't work here." 

They're not lying. They're protecting themselves from a transition they'll have to manage while hitting the same KPIs.

I've learned to separate the evaluation into two distinct conversations:

**First conversation:** If you had no history, no relationships, no installed processes—who would you choose? Get that answer clean, on paper, before anyone talks about "practicalities."

**Second conversation:** What would the transition actually require? Time, resources, risk mitigation. Put real numbers on it.

What you'll find is that the switching cost, properly scoped, is almost always smaller than the compound cost of staying with an underperformer. A 15% savings over a five-year contract easily funds a dedicated transition manager for six months.

The other thing I do now: I tell incumbent vendors during the RFP that we've already budgeted for transition costs. Takes away their favourite negotiating lever—"you know how painful switching would be."

Watch their pricing suddenly become more competitive when they realise inertia isn't protecting them anymore.

The best procurement decisions I've made came from treating switching costs as a solvable problem rather than an immovable constraint. Your team's comfort with the current state is a data point, not a veto.
