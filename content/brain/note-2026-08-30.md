There's a counterintuitive problem I've noticed in procurement: the more intelligence you gather on a supplier, the more likely you are to negotiate against yourself.

When you know their margins, their capacity constraints, their competing bids, their internal politics — you start building their case in your head. You anticipate their objections before they raise them. You soften your opening position because you "know" what they'll accept.

I've watched skilled procurement professionals talk themselves out of aggressive positions in pre-meeting prep sessions. "They'll never go below X because we know their COGS." "They're losing the Dubai contract so they need us, but not enough to accept Y."

The information becomes a ceiling instead of a floor.

The fix isn't ignorance — intelligence is still essential. It's separating the gathering phase from the positioning phase. Gather everything. Then, when you sit down to negotiate, let them tell you what's possible. Make them work. Your intelligence should inform your walk-away point and help you detect bluffs, not pre-compromise your ask.

In ground handling contracts, I've seen this play out repeatedly. We'll know a handler is desperate for a new airline contract to utilise spare equipment. Armed with that knowledge, the temptation is to offer something "reasonable" that we're certain they'll accept. But reasonable isn't optimal. Starting at reasonable means finishing below it.

The best negotiators I've worked with hold their intelligence lightly. They know the landscape but negotiate as if they don't. They create space for the other party to surprise them — and sometimes they do, in your favour.

Information is leverage. But only if you don't use it to negotiate with yourself before the meeting starts.
