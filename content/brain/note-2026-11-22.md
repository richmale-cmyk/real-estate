I've seen procurement rounds collapse not because vendors were incompetent, but because we specified ourselves into a corner.

The instinct makes sense: you've been burned before, so you write tighter specs. You nail down every deliverable, every timeline, every penalty clause. The document grows to 47 pages. Legal is happy. And then you wonder why you're getting bids from vendors who are excellent at compliance but mediocre at solving your actual problem.

Ground handling equipment is a perfect example. I've inherited contracts where the RFP specified the exact manufacturer, model number, and maintenance schedule for baggage tractors — but said nothing about uptime guarantees during peak operations. We got exactly what we asked for: compliant equipment that sat broken during Hajj season while the vendor pointed to the maintenance schedule they were following to the letter.

The fix isn't looser specs. It's relocating the precision.

Now I specify outcomes obsessively: 98.7% equipment availability during published peak windows. Response time to AOG situations. Penalty structures that actually hurt. But I leave the *how* deliberately open. Tell me what equipment you'll use and why — that's your expertise, not mine.

This flips the vendor relationship. Instead of them gaming compliance checkboxes, they're forced to demonstrate they understand the operation. The ones who can't articulate a coherent equipment strategy self-select out before evaluation even begins.

The other benefit: you learn something. The best vendors have seen problems you haven't encountered yet. When you over-specify the solution, you forfeit access to that knowledge.

Procurement isn't about control. It's about creating the conditions where good vendors can differentiate themselves from paper-compliant ones.
