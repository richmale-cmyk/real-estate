Aircraft don't fly continuously until they break. They have mandatory maintenance windows — scheduled, non-negotiable time where they're taken out of service to be inspected, repaired, and returned to airworthiness.

I've started applying this thinking to myself.

For years I ran like an A321 on a tight rotation — maximum utilization, minimum ground time. The logic seemed sound: more flight hours, more value extracted. But aircraft maintenance exists because we know that continuous operation degrades systems in ways that aren't always visible until something fails catastrophically.

Now I schedule maintenance windows. Not vacations — those are different. I mean dedicated time blocks where I'm explicitly not producing output. Where I'm reviewing my own systems: what's working, what's showing wear, what needs replacement before it fails.

The parallels run deeper than you'd expect. Just like we have A-checks, B-checks, C-checks with increasing depth and duration, I've built tiers:

- Daily checks: 20 minutes reviewing what's consuming my attention
- Weekly checks: Two hours examining my commitments and energy allocation  
- Quarterly checks: Full days where I question fundamental assumptions about how I'm operating

The hardest part isn't finding the time. It's resisting the pressure — internal and external — to maximize utilization. There's always another flight that could be added to the schedule.

But I've seen what happens to operators who defer maintenance to chase short-term metrics. The unscheduled downtime eventually comes, and it's always more expensive than the scheduled kind would have been.

You can't inspect yourself while you're at 35,000 feet. You have to come down, power off the engines, and look at what's actually happening underneath the panels.
