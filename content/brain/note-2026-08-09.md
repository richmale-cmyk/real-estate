I've watched vendors love the perpetual pilot. They get revenue, reference logos, and zero pressure to prove ROI. Meanwhile, we get comfortable with "testing" something for eighteen months while the actual decision calcifies into the default.

In ground operations, this shows up constantly. A new baggage reconciliation system. A passenger flow analytics tool. A predictive maintenance platform. The pilot starts small — one station, limited scope, friendly metrics. Then it quietly expands. Two stations. Five. Suddenly you're paying real money for something that was never formally procured, never competitively benchmarked, and never held to the standards you'd apply if you'd run proper diligence.

The original sin is treating pilots as low-stakes. They're not. Every pilot is a procurement decision wearing casual clothes.

Here's what I now require before any pilot begins:

1. **Exit criteria defined upfront.** What specific, measurable outcomes mean we stop? Not "if it doesn't work" — actual numbers.
2. **Sunset date in writing.** Twelve weeks maximum. No extensions without formal review.
3. **Competitive awareness.** Before piloting vendor A, I want to know why we're not piloting vendors B or C. Sometimes the answer is good. Sometimes it reveals lazy sourcing.
4. **Total cost projection.** Not pilot cost — full deployment cost. If we can't afford the real thing, why are we testing it?

The hardest part is political. Someone championed this pilot. They've told their team it's promising. Killing it feels like killing their judgment. But that's exactly why the criteria need to exist before emotions get involved.

A pilot should accelerate decisions, not defer them. If you've been "evaluating" something for a year, you haven't been evaluating. You've been avoiding.
