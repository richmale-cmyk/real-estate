I've watched vendors pitch "proof of concept" projects like they're doing us a favour. Free trial here, pilot program there. Sounds generous until you realise what's actually happening: we're building their case study while they delay our procurement decision indefinitely.

The perpetual pilot is a trap dressed as prudence.

Here's the pattern. A vendor offers a 90-day pilot at Station X. We invest operational attention, staff training, workflow adjustments. At day 85, results are "promising but inconclusive." They recommend extending to three more stations. Six months later, we've proven their product works in our environment — using our people, our time, our institutional knowledge — and now we're too invested to walk away.

The vendor got free market validation. We got dependency without negotiated terms.

I now treat pilot proposals like I treat scope creep: with structural skepticism. Before any pilot begins, I require three things in writing:

1. **Exit criteria** — What specific outcome ends this pilot, pass or fail?
2. **Transition terms** — If we proceed, what are the already-agreed commercial parameters?
3. **Kill date** — An absolute deadline, not extendable without senior sign-off.

The hardest discipline is killing a pilot that's "working okay." Okay isn't a procurement strategy. Okay is how you end up with seventeen different ground handling systems across your network because no one ever made a real decision.

Pilots should be hypothesis tests, not emotional commitments. You're not dating the vendor. You're evaluating whether their solution beats your next-best alternative at a price that makes operational sense.

Every pilot without an exit plan is just a slow-motion yes.
