In aviation, we understand that an aircraft requires scheduled maintenance whether it flew that day or not. Corrosion doesn't wait. Hydraulic seals degrade. The calendar is as much an enemy as flight cycles.

But in every other domain I've worked in — procurement systems, team structures, AI agents, personal workflows — people act surprised when things decay. They build something that works, declare victory, and move on. Six months later they're rebuilding from scratch, wondering what went wrong.

Nothing went wrong. Everything just stopped being maintained.

I've started applying what I call the maintenance mindset outside of aircraft. When I build an AI agent system, I block time monthly to review its decision logs. Not because something broke — because drift is invisible until it's catastrophic. When I design a new vendor management process, I build in quarterly reviews from day one. The review isn't a response to problems; it's the system working correctly.

The hard part isn't accepting this intellectually. It's protecting maintenance time when everything feels fine. When the operation is humming, the pressure is always to redeploy resources to new initiatives. Maintenance feels like overhead. It feels like pessimism.

But here's what operations teaches you: the absence of visible problems is often the presence of invisible debt accumulating. The smoothest months are frequently the ones generating next quarter's crisis.

What I tell my station managers: your job isn't to fix problems. Your job is to prevent the conditions that create them. That requires scheduled intervention on systems that appear healthy.

The question I ask myself now before launching anything: What's the maintenance protocol? If I can't answer it, I'm not building — I'm just creating future archaeology.
