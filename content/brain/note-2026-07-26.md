There's a pattern I've watched play out dozens of times in ground handling procurement: the more precisely you specify what you want, the fewer vendors can bid, and the more you pay.

This seems counterintuitive. Shouldn't clarity be good? Yes — but there's a difference between clarity of outcome and over-prescription of method.

When you write an RFP that demands a specific brand of ground power unit, a particular software platform, or an exact staffing model, you've just eliminated 70% of your potential suppliers. The remaining 30% know it. They price accordingly.

I've learned to write requirements around performance outcomes instead. "Turnaround in 35 minutes with 99.2% on-time performance" opens the field. Let vendors compete on how they'll achieve it. You often discover approaches you hadn't considered — some better than what you would have mandated.

The exception is integration requirements. If a piece of equipment needs to interface with existing systems, that's not over-specification, that's physics. But even then, I push back on my own technical teams: is this integration truly necessary, or have we just always done it this way?

The other trap is specifying current state rather than future state. If you're procuring a five-year ground handling contract, write requirements for where your operation will be in year three, not where it is today. I've seen stations locked into contracts that couldn't scale because someone optimised for present-day flight frequencies.

Specificity feels like control. But in procurement, it's often just the illusion of control traded for real money. The discipline is knowing which details actually matter — and having the confidence to leave everything else open.
