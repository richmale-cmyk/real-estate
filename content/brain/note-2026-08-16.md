I've watched smart operations leaders lose millions by writing procurement specs that are too tight.

The instinct makes sense. You've been burned before. The last vendor delivered garbage. So this time you specify everything: exact equipment models, precise response times, specific staff-to-flight ratios, detailed penalty matrices. You feel protected.

But here's what actually happens. Vendors who could solve your problem creatively walk away because they can't meet one arbitrary line item. The ones who remain price in every specification as a risk premium. And when reality diverges from your spec — which it always does — you're stuck renegotiating from weakness because the contract says what it says.

The better approach: specify outcomes relentlessly, methods loosely.

I need 98% of flights to depart within their slot window. I need passenger complaints below X per thousand. I need damage claims under Y annually. How you achieve that is your expertise — that's why I'm hiring you.

This does two things. It attracts vendors who actually know ground handling, not just vendors who know how to tick compliance boxes. And it gives you leverage throughout the contract because the conversation stays focused on results, not process arguments.

The exception: specify methods tightly when failure modes are catastrophic or when you genuinely know the domain better than any vendor could. Safety equipment standards, for instance. Non-negotiable.

But for most operational procurement, your job isn't to design the vendor's operation. Your job is to define success so clearly that a competent partner can't hide from it.

Loose specs with tight accountability beats tight specs with loose accountability every time.
